Featured Project · Product Design, Fall 2025

Precise Spice

A spice container with an integrated measuring system

Carnegie Mellon University · September – December 2025 · Team of five

  • SolidWorks
  • Mechanism Design
  • Tolerancing
  • Prototyping
View poster →

My role: I designed the mechanism that drives each dispense, from the thumb dial and lead screw that set the amount to the slider and belt-and-pulley drive that turn the measuring drum. I also toleranced the assembly so all of it fits in a container the size of a standard spice jar. I was also the team's prototype production lead.

Overview

Measuring spoons often go unused in the kitchen: they add dishes, and larger spoons don't fit inside spice jars. Precise Spice lets you set and dispense an exact amount straight from the jar in two steps. Turn a dial to choose the amount, then press a slider tab to dispense it.

Research

We shadowed three home cooks, surveyed about 20 people, and ran a use study with the main competitor. Participants owned around 40 spices on average, yet none measured while cooking. Survey respondents did measure when baking, and pointed to dishes, mess, and spoons that don't fit in jars as the reasons they skip it.

The only mass-market competitor, KitchenArt's Select-A-Spice, dispenses just ¼ tsp at a time: a tablespoon means twelve turns of a knob. Users found it unintuitive and doubted its accuracy.

That defined our targets:

  • Intuitive to use without instructions
  • Multiple increments, ¼ tsp to 1 tbsp, in a single operation
  • Fits the drawers, cabinets and racks people already use for spices
  • Airtight, so spices stay fresh

Five Concepts

We sketched five measuring mechanisms, each able to dispense every target increment, and scored them in a Pugh chart against a standard measuring spoon.

The winning idea counts out fixed ¼ tsp portions rather than resizing one measuring chamber. Since a tablespoon is twelve times a quarter teaspoon, a chamber that grows to fit it would have added bulk. Counting portions kept the container small, which set up the core challenge of my part: a mechanism that can count anywhere from 1 to 12 portions in one push, inside a jar-sized body.

The Dispensing Mechanism

In the final design, the rack became a belt-and-pulley drive paired with a lead screw that sets its travel:

  1. Set: the user turns a knurled dial at the base with their thumb. It rotates a lead screw, which moves a stop, marked with an indicator, up or down to the chosen increment.
  2. Dispense: the user presses the slider tab down until it meets the stop. The belt converts that straight-line travel into rotation of a pulley on the drum's axle.
  3. Measure: the drum has eight ¼ tsp partitions. Each partition that rotates past the opening drops exactly one portion.

Sizing the drive

A tablespoon is 12 partitions, or 1.5 drum rotations. That set the slider travel and the belt length:

Pulley diameterdp = 0.382 in
Slider travel for 1 tbspΔs = 1.5πdp = 1.8 in
Minimum belt lengthlb = 2Δs + πdp = 4.8 in

Because every increment uses the same press, just with a different stop height, a tablespoon takes about as long as a quarter teaspoon. That fixes the competitor's twelve-turn problem.

A Tight Form Factor

The container had to sit on an existing spice rack, so the whole mechanism had to fit in a 1.8″ × 3.2″ × 4″ body alongside 7.09 in³ of spice. I packaged the lead screw and belt as a slim vertical column beside the hopper, with the pulley sitting directly on the drum's axle. That way the drive adds almost no width.

Shrinking it, iteration by iteration

Getting there took multiple prototypes. Each one significantly decreased the form factor: from an early rack-and-pinion proof of concept, to a belt-and-pulley prototype, to the final compact design.

Well toleranced

With so little room, there was no slack to hide sloppy fits. I toleranced each interface so parts move freely without rattling, binding or leaking spice:

  • The drum within its housing, so partitions hold an accurate ¼ tsp and spice can't slip past
  • The slider in its track, for a smooth press with no wobble
  • The stop on the lead screw, so each increment lands in the same place every time
  • Pulley center distance against belt length, so the belt stays tensioned without slipping

Drum & Hopper

A refillable sheet-metal hopper funnels spice into the segmented drum. We sized the drum so each of its eight partitions holds exactly ¼ tsp (0.0752 in³), which gives an outer diameter of 1.014 in.
